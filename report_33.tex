% ── 1. Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
Social media platforms, like Twitter/X, have become a primary channel through which
retail and institutional investors express and discover market sentiment. Automated classification
of financial tweets into Bearish, Bullish and Neutral stances enables real-time signal generation
for algorithmic trading, risk monitoring and investor-sentiment tracking (\cite{antweiler2004talk} \cite{bollen2011twitter} ).

This work addresses multiclass financial sentiment classification - a text-categorization
problem. We explore the full NLP pipeline, from raw text through feature engineering to
model selection and hyperparameter tuning. All
models are trained on the provided training corpus. The remainder of the report is
organised as follows: Section~2 explores the dataset; Section~3 analyses preprocessing; Section~4
describes feature engineering; Section~5 presents the classification models; Section~6 reports results and analysis; Section~7
details the agentic workflow \textbf{(Extra Challenge 2)}; Section~8 concludes.

% ── 2. Data Exploration ──────────────────────────────────────────

\section{Data Exploration}

The training set contains 9{,}543 labelled tweets across three sentiment classes (Bearish,
Bullish, Neutral) and 299 unlabelled test tweets. A quality gate confirmed no missing
values, no exact duplicates and no exact train/test overlap, so no imputation or
deduplication was required.

\subsection{Class Distribution}

Neutral dominates at 64.7\%, Bullish at 20.2\% and Bearish at only 15.1\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:classdist}), yielding a Neutral-to-Bearish ratio of 4.28:1. This
imbalance has three direct methodological consequences: macro-averaged F1 is adopted as
the primary metric, class-weighted objectives are used for all classifiers and all
cross-validation folds are stratified to preserve the original class proportions.

\subsection{Tweet Length Analysis}

All three classes share nearly identical length distributions, with a mean of approximately
12 words, a median of 11 and a corpus maximum of 32 (Figure~\ref{fig:lengthdist}). Length
is therefore not a discriminating feature and is not added as an auxiliary input. A
Kolmogorov--Smirnov test on character length and word count finds no statistically
significant difference between training and test distributions ($p = 0.30$ and
$p = 0.45$, respectively; Figure~\ref{fig:distribshift}) and adversarial validation
yields AUC = 0.486, confirming the absence of distributional shift and validating
cross-validation estimates as reliable proxies for held-out test performance.

\subsection{Financial Twitter Structural Features}

Bullish tweets carry the highest cashtag density (mean 0.34 per tweet), followed by
Bearish (0.22), while Neutral lags notably behind (0.17), consistent with directional
analyst commentary targeting specific stocks (Figure~\ref{fig:structural}). URLs appear
in roughly 47\% of tweets across all classes and function primarily as structural metadata
rather than sentiment signals.

\subsection{Cashtag and Hashtag Frequency Analysis}

The corpus spans a broad range of US equities, with energy, technology and banking sectors
dominating cashtag mentions and following a long-tailed frequency distribution
(Figure~\ref{fig:cashtagfreq}).

\subsection{Cashtag Sentiment Breakdown}

For most tickers, one sentiment class dominates (Figure~\ref{fig:cashtagsentiment}),
though large-cap names show a more balanced split depending on surrounding analyst
language. A cashtag identity ablation comparing intact cashtags, a generic
\texttt{TICKER} token and full cashtag removal finds macro-F1 within one standard
deviation across all three configurations (0.7149 to 0.7161): ticker identity carries no
sentiment signal for sparse models. Raw cashtags are nonetheless retained for transformer
models to remain within their pre-training distribution.

\subsection{Vocabulary Analysis and Discriminative Terms}

Word clouds (Figure~\ref{fig:wordclouds}) confirm the expected signal: Bearish is
dominated by downgrade language (\textit{cut}, \textit{drop}, \textit{miss}); Bullish by
positive analyst actions (\textit{raised}, \textit{beats}, \textit{growth}); Neutral by
reporting verbs (\textit{announces}, \textit{reports}) without directional framing.
Bigram and trigram analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:ngrams}) reveals that phrase-level patterns
carry cleaner signal than unigrams: Bearish includes \textit{price target cut} and
\textit{reiterate sell}; Bullish mirrors with \textit{price target raised} and
\textit{beats estimates}. Both classes share the same syntactic template but with opposite
polarity, so a model that fails to capture directionality will systematically confuse
them. Mean TF-IDF scores (Figure~\ref{fig:tfidf}) confirm clean term-level separation,
with Bearish associated with \textit{misses}, \textit{lower}, \textit{cut},
\textit{china} and \textit{coronavirus}; Bullish with \textit{beats}, \textit{target},
\textit{price target} and \textit{revenue}; and Neutral with \textit{results},
\textit{dividend}, \textit{reports} and \textit{declares}. A Venn diagram analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig:venn}), however, shows that roughly half of the top vocabulary is shared
across all three classes: the hard cases are not unknown words but the same words used in
opposite contexts (e.g.\ \textit{``raised guidance''} vs.\ \textit{``raised concerns''}),
precisely where bag-of-words representations fail and contextual models excel.

\subsection{VADER Baseline Analysis}

Applying VADER (Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner) to all training tweets
yields approximately 48\% accuracy, well below the 64.7\% majority-class baseline
(Figure~\ref{fig:vader}). Financial analyst language is factual and understated rather
than emotionally explicit, causing VADER to systematically map directional statements to
Neutral. This directly motivates the use of domain-adapted encoders such as FinBERT and
Twitter-RoBERTa over general-purpose sentiment tools.

\subsection{Encoding Artefacts}

A character-level audit detects UTF-8 mojibake in 7.0\% of the corpus, with Neutral
carrying the highest rate (8.7\%), followed by Bearish (5.5\%) and Bullish (2.9\%). This
asymmetry is self-reinforcing: the dominant class already pulls predictions toward Neutral
and additional noise in its tweets pushes ambiguous cases further in that direction.
Corrupted apostrophes also cause subword tokenisers to split tokens such as
\textit{Moody's} into spurious character sequences, wasting token slots without
contributing signal. These findings motivate the inclusion of an \texttt{ftfy} text repair
step in the preprocessing pipeline.

\subsection{Near-Duplicate Audit}

No exact duplicates were found in the training set. After normalisation, 201
near-duplicate rows (2.11\%) were identified, representing URL or encoding variants of
legitimate tweets rather than true duplicates, so no rows are dropped. Three
near-duplicate groups carry conflicting labels across different annotations, providing
direct evidence of annotation noise. Additionally, 42 train/test near-overlapping tweets
were identified, a property of the externally provided split that cannot be altered.

\subsection{Label Noise Estimation}

An 8-encoder ensemble flags instances where high-confidence predictions disagree with the
label. At a 95\% confidence threshold, this covers 46 tweets (approximately 0.48\%). A
representative example, ``\$WING -- Baird returns to Wingstop bull camp'', is labelled
Neutral yet predicted Bullish at 0.97 confidence, illustrating systematic annotation
inconsistency at the Bullish/Neutral boundary. This provides a practical lower bound on
the irreducible error and sets a ceiling on achievable macro-F1 regardless of model
capacity.


\section{Data Preprocessing}

\subsection{Corpus Split and Validation Protocol}

The labelled corpus comprises 9{,}543 tweets. Because no held-out validation set was
provided alongside the training data, all performance estimates are obtained through
stratified k-fold cross-validation. A single holdout partition was deliberately avoided:
the minority classes, Bearish (15.1\%) and Bullish (20.2\%), are too infrequent for a
fixed split to yield stable macro-F1 estimates, particularly for the Bearish class, which
constitutes only around 1{,}440 samples in total. Stratified k-fold partitioning, which
preserves the Bearish/Bullish/Neutral class distribution in every fold, was therefore the
most appropriate choice given the size and imbalance profile of this corpus.

Two protocols are employed, differentiated by model family. Classical ML models (BoW,
TF-IDF, Word2Vec, frozen contextual embeddings, LightGBM and all ablation probes),
transformer encoder Phase~2 fine-tuning, ensembles and knowledge distillation all use
\textbf{10-fold} stratified cross-validation
(\texttt{StratifiedKFold(n\_splits=10, shuffle=True, random\_state=42)}), giving training
partitions of approximately 8{,}589 samples and validation partitions of approximately
954. Two lighter-weight experiments use \textbf{5-fold}: the Phase~1 backbone screening
pass (Section~\ref{sec:encoders}), which evaluates seven candidate architectures with a
reduced training budget before committing to full 10-fold runs and the GPT-2 decoder
experiments (Section~\ref{sec:gpt2}), which served as a contrastive probe rather than a
submission candidate. The choice of 10-fold for the primary pipeline reflects the
imbalanced minority classes: smaller validation folds (954 samples) improve macro-F1
stability for the Bearish class while remaining tractable under GPU acceleration. The
5-fold exceptions balance variance reduction against the additional wall-clock cost of
configurations that were exploratory rather than submission-critical.

In both protocols, out-of-fold (OOF) predictions cover the full training corpus without
data leakage, since each prediction is generated by a model that never trained on that
sample. The global random seed (42) is fixed across Python, NumPy and PyTorch, ensuring
all OOF estimates are directly comparable across model families. Class proportions are
preserved in every fold to within 0.1 percentage points, confirming that stratification
is effective across the full range of partition sizes.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing Pipeline}

The preprocessing pipeline is implemented in \texttt{src/preprocessing.py} and shared
across all model families, though not applied identically to all of them. The pipeline
does not constitute a single fixed strategy: rather, different feature representations
receive different subsets of the available steps, as detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}.

\subsubsection{Motivation}

Three findings from the exploratory analysis directly informed the pipeline design.
Approximately 75\% of tweets carry no strongly polarised lexical signal, meaning that
aggressive token removal risks discarding the contextual cues that distinguish ambiguous
financial language. The Bearish class, being the hardest to classify, relies
disproportionately on negation constructions, so a standard stopword list that removes
\texttt{not}, \texttt{no}, or \texttt{never} would invert the sentiment of expressions
such as ``not bullish'' or ``can't sustain growth,'' directly harming Bearish recall.
Finally, cashtags and hashtags exhibit class-specific distributional differences
identified during the EDA and their surface forms carry structural information that
should be preserved rather than discarded.

\subsubsection{Techniques}

Six preprocessing techniques are implemented, each motivated by the characteristics of
the domain. \textbf{Twitter noise cleaning (regex)}, applied via regular expressions, maps URLs and user
mentions to placeholder tokens and converts cashtags to structured identifiers (e.g.\
\texttt{\$TSLA} to \texttt{TICKER\_TSLA}), preserving ticker identity without introducing
raw symbols into the vocabulary. \textbf{Unicode normalisation (NFKD + ASCII)}, using NFKD decomposition followed
by ASCII transliteration, resolves curly quotes, bullet characters and non-ASCII currency
symbols that would otherwise fragment the vocabulary across visually identical surface
forms. \textbf{Stop-word removal} is applied with a negation preservation whitelist: contractions
are first expanded (e.g.\ \texttt{n't} to \texttt{not}, \texttt{don't} to \texttt{do not})
before filtering and tokens such as \texttt{not}, \texttt{no}, \texttt{never},
\texttt{neither} and \texttt{nor} are explicitly excluded from removal. Tokenisation uses
NLTK's \texttt{TweetTokenizer}, which handles tweet-specific punctuation and emoticons
while operating on the noise-cleaned text. Two morphological reduction strategies are also
implemented as alternatives: \texttt{WordNet lemmatisation} and \texttt{Snowball stemming}. Lemmatisation is
preferred for dense representations because stemming tends to over-compress financially
relevant terms, collapsing distinct roots that carry different sentiment signals. Both are
evaluated empirically in the ablation below.


\subsubsection{Vocabulary Reduction}

To quantify the pipeline's effect on sparsity, 500 tweets per class were sampled from the
training set and passed through the full pipeline. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:vocabreduction} (token count across preprocessing stages and vocabulary
reduction by class), the vocabulary reduction is broadly
consistent across Bearish, Bullish and Neutral tweets, which is expected given that the
pipeline is class-agnostic by design. Any class-specific effects, such as Bearish tweets
retaining more tokens due to negation preservation, are attributable to content
differences rather than to the pipeline itself.

\subsubsection{Preprocessing Ablation}

A preliminary ablation evaluates five preprocessing configurations using a fixed probe
classifier, Logistic Regression with a 10{,}000-feature TF-IDF vocabulary, under the
leak-free 5-fold cross-validation protocol, with the vectoriser fitted inside each fold.
The results, summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} (Preprocessing Ablation Study), show a narrow but consistent ordering: light cleaning alone
(``clean only'', macro-F1 = 0.7179) outperforms all more aggressive variants, with the
addition of stopword removal and lemmatisation reducing performance to 0.7068 and
stemming reducing it further to 0.7062. Applying no preprocessing at all yields 0.7152,
confirming that noise cleaning provides a small but consistent benefit while further
morphological reduction is counterproductive for this corpus. This pattern aligns with the
EDA observation that financially relevant vocabulary, including tickers, event verbs and
analyst terminology, carries discriminative signal that aggressive normalisation tends to
suppress. The differences across configurations are small and within one standard
deviation, so no strong causal claim is drawn from this probe alone.

\section{Feature Engineering}

Feature engineering converts each tweet into a numerical vector that a classifier can process.
Eleven representations were built across four families, progressing from sparse and interpretable
baselines to rich contextual embeddings.

\subsection{Sparse Representations (BoW and TF-IDF)}

Both BoW and TF-IDF represent each tweet as a vector with one position per vocabulary word.
Binary BoW records word presence/absence and serves as an interpretable lower bound.
TF-IDF improves on this by weighting each word by how distinctive it is across the corpus:
rare financial terms like \textit{downgrade} or \textit{beats} receive high weight while common
words are suppressed. The unigram+bigram variant adds two-word sequences to capture financial
expressions such as \textit{price target} or \textit{earnings beat} that vanish at the word
level. The character 3–5gram variant operates on overlapping character sequences, making the
representation robust to Twitter spelling noise (\textit{bullllish} and \textit{bullish} share
most character sequences and produce similar vectors). All sparse methods share a fundamental
limitation: they treat words independently, with no notion of order or context.

\subsection{Static Word Embeddings (Word2Vec and GloVe)}

Word2Vec learns a dense vector per word by training a neural network to predict words from
their context, so semantically related words end up numerically close : \textit{bullish} and
\textit{optimistic} become neighbours in the vector space. Skip-gram predicts context from the
centre word and handles rare financial terms better; CBOW predicts the centre word from context
and is stronger on frequent vocabulary. Both 200-dimensional models are trained on the 9,543
in-domain tweets. GloVe-Twitter-100, pre-trained on 2 billion tweets,
provides broader coverage of informal Twitter language at the cost of financial specificity.
Each tweet is represented by mean pooling its token vectors. The key remaining limitation is
context-insensitivity: the vector for \textit{cut} is identical in \textit{Fed cuts rates} and
\textit{analyst cuts target}, despite opposite implications.

\subsection{Contextual Transformer CLS Embeddings}

Transformer encoders solve context-insensitivity by reading the entire sequence through
self-attention, where each word's representation is shaped by all other words in the tweet.
A special \texttt{[CLS]} token accumulates this information across all encoder layers, producing
a 768-dimensional contextualised summary of the tweet. Three encoders
are used in frozen mode (weights not updated) as pure feature extractors.

\textbf{FinBERT} \cite{araci2019finbert}, fine-tuned on financial news and earnings reports, provides
direct domain alignment for financial vocabulary and sentiment. However, its training corpus
is formal text, which limits its ability to handle the informal language, abbreviations and
noise typical of Twitter.

\textbf{SBERT all-mpnet-base-v2} \cite{reimers2019sentencebert} is trained with contrastive
objectives, producing embeddings well-suited for similarity-based classifiers — however, it
has no alignment with either financial text or Twitter language.
\textbf{This constitutes Extra Work \#1 under the Feature Engineering criterion.}

\textbf{Twitter-RoBERTa} \cite{barbieri2020tweeteval}, pre-trained on 58M tweets, handles informal
language, abbreviations and the noisy writing style typical of Twitter — however, its
pre-training corpus is general Twitter data, not financial text specifically, so it may miss
domain-specific vocabulary.
\textbf{This constitutes \textbf{Extra Work} \#2 under the Feature Engineering criterion.}

PCA projections of the three encoder spaces confirmed that FinBERT (47.1\% variance explained)
and Twitter-RoBERTa (52.0\%) produce visibly cleaner class separation than SBERT (7.7\%).

\subsection{Financial Hand-crafted Features}

A 14-dimensional vector captures Twitter surface signals that no unsupervised embedding learns
by design: VADER sentiment scores (\textit{pos}, \textit{neg}, \textit{neu}, \textit{compound};
\cite{hutto2014vader}), cashtag, hashtag, mention and URL counts, word and character count,
exclamation and question marks, all-caps ratio and Flesch readability score. A fusion
experiment concatenating these features with SBERT embeddings (782 dimensions total) yielded
a marginal gain of +0.0032 F1-macro, suggesting SBERT already captures most surface signal
implicitly : a useful negative result that informed the final feature-set decision.

% ── 5. Classification Models ─────────────────────────────────────
\section{Classification Models}
The classification experiments follow a deliberate progression from simple to complex, with each stage motivated by the limitations of the preceding one. Four model families are evaluated: classical machine learning models over engineered feature sets, transformer encoders used first as frozen feature extractors and then fine-tuned end-to-end, a decoder-based classifier included as a contrastive experiment and an ensemble of the strongest fine-tuned encoders. Sections 5.3 and 5.5 constitute additional exploratory work beyond the core submission.




\subsection{Traditional ML}
We pair each model family with the feature representations it suits best.
Sparse models (NB/LR/SVM) run on BoW and TF-IDF (1g/2g/char);
dense models run on Word2Vec, GloVe and frozen encoder embeddings (FinBERT/SBERT/RoBERTa):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \tH{Na\"ive Bayes:} MultinomialNB ($\alpha=1.0$) and ComplementNB ($\alpha=0.5$)
    on sparse features; ComplementNB handles class imbalance better (\cite{rennie2003tackling}).
  \item \tH{Logistic Regression:} $L_2$ (SAGA), $C \in \{0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$, all feature
    sets; a C-tuned variant via Optuna ($C^*=8.86$) on TF--IDF 2g is also included.
  \item \tH{Linear SVM:} \texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV(LinearSVC)}, $C \in \{0.1, 1.0\}$,
    sparse features.
  \item \tH{KNN:} $k \in \{5, 15\}$, cosine distance, dense embeddings.
  \item \tH{MLP:} $(256,128)$, ReLU, early stopping, dense embeddings.
  \item \tH{Random Forest / XGBoost / LightGBM:} 100 and 300 estimators; LightGBM is the
    strongest traditional learner on dense features and is additionally tuned with
    \textbf{Optuna} (\cite{akiba2019optuna}) - TPE sampler, median pruner, 50 trials - on
    frozen RoBERTa embeddings.
\end{itemize}

All 68 classical (model~$\times$~feature) combinations were produced by \texttt{`scripts/run\_classical\_ml.py`} through the shared \texttt{`evaluate\_model`} routine in \texttt{`src/evaluation.py`} under 10-fold stratified cross-validation. The two Optuna-tuned configurations are described in \texttt{`scripts/run\_optuna\_tuning.py`}. All results are  consolidated in \texttt{results/tables/classical\_ml\_full.csv}. These results define the non-neural performance boundary and motivate end-to-end fine-tuning, where the encoder weights themselves adapt to the task's label space.

\subsection{Transformer Encoders for Classification}
\label{sec:encoders}
Whereas before we used the encoders only as \emph{frozen} embeddings, here the full
encoder is fine-tuned end-to-end (\cite{devlin2019bert}). Backbone and configuration selection followed a deliberate two-phase process.

\textbf{Phase 1 - Backbone screening:} A lightweight 5-fold pass
(AdamW, cosine schedule, class-weighted CE, fp16, 4\,ep, lr\,$=2\!\times\!10^{-5}$,
maxlen\,96) was run over seven candidate architectures to identify the most promising families without committing to the cost of full 10-fold training. FinBERT-fintwitter (`nickmuchi/finbert-tone-finetuned-fintwitter-classification`) was entered directly into the 10-fold phase as the primary domain-matched backbone, given its direct pre-training alignment with financial Twitter text.
Two patterns emerged: larger models consistently outperform their base equivalents and
domain-specific pre-training matters - \texttt{ProsusAI/finbert} scored $\approx7$\,pp
below the fintwitter variant on this exact task. Among the screened backbones, Twitter-RoBERTa-large-topic-sentiment was retained;
all base-size models and weaker large models were dropped.

\textbf{Phase 2 - Configuration tuning:} Eight encoder configurations were trained under the full 10-fold stratified protocol, covering four backbone families with systematic variation of epochs, learning rate and the `fix\_text` preprocessing flag. The shared training protocol used AdamW with a cosine schedule and linear warmup, gradient clipping at 1.0, fp16 mixed precision, class-weighted cross-entropy with label smoothing of 0.05 and best-checkpoint selection per fold on validation macro-F1. DeBERTa-v3-large required gradient accumulation (effective batch size 32) and a longer warmup ratio (10\% versus 6\%) due to its depth; an initial run without fp16 GradScaler collapsed to single-class prediction and was discarded. \subsection{Decoder Model - GPT-2}
\label{sec:gpt2}
All models in Section~5.2 are \emph{encoders} (BERT-family, bidirectional): they read the
full tweet simultaneously. \textbf{Decoder} models (GPT-family, causal/autoregressive) were
designed for text generation. Fine-tuning a decoder for classification is therefore a deliberate contrastive experiment, measuring the value of the bidirectional inductive bias on this task.

Two settings are compared under the 5-fold protocol:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \tH{Fine-tuned classifier.} \texttt{GPT2ForSequenceClassification} pools the last
    non-padding token into a 3-class linear head; all parameters are trained end-to-end
    (4\,ep, lr\,$=2\!\times\!10^{-5}$, batch\,16, class-weighted CE, linear warmup\,10\,\%).
    EOS is reused as the padding token since GPT-2 has no native pad.
  \item \tH{Few-shot prompting.} Three labelled examples per class are prepended to a new
    tweet with no parameter updates (contextual calibration \cite{zhao2021calibrate}). This is
    a lower bound on what an untuned decoder achieves.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ensembles}
\label{sec:ensemble}
The eight fine-tuned encoders from Section~5.2 were chosen deliberately to span different
pre-training domains (financial-Twitter, generic-Twitter, general-finance), architectures
(BERT/RoBERTa/DeBERTa) and text preprocessing regimes. This diversity produces partially
uncorrelated errors: tweets that one model misclassifies are often correctly classified by
another and averaging probability vectors smooths out individual failures. GPT-2 is excluded as its performance sits below the weakest encoder and its inclusion would dilute rather than improve the aggregate signal. Three strategies were compared on the 10-fold OOF probability vectors of the fine-tuned encoders:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \tH{Simple soft-voting:} Probability vectors averaged with equal weights or weights
    proportional to individual OOF F1.
  \item \tH{Stacked generalisation:} OOF probability vectors concatenated
into an 18-dimensional meta-feature matrix; a Logistic-Regression meta-learner trained on top (5-fold, leakage-free). Five models whose OOF files were available at the time  were used as base learners. \cite{wolpert1992stacked})
  \item \tH{Optimised weighted soft-voting;} Coordinate-ascent over
    $\{0.25,\,0.5,\,0.75,\,1.0\}^8$: each model's weight is updated one at a time, keeping
    any change that improves OOF macro-F1, until convergence.
\end{itemize}

Five fine-tuned encoders served as base learners; this strategy was later superseded by optimised soft-voting over all eight models. The optimal weights assign 1.0 to four models, 0.75 to three and 0.5 to the most architecture-distinct member (\texttt{deberta-base-finance-fixtext}). All eight members
receive raw tweet text directly to their own transformer tokeniser -
\textbf{no BoW, Word2Vec, or GloVe features are used}; the only input-side variation
is whether \texttt{fix\_text} encoding repair is applied. Table~\ref{tab:ensemble_members}
lists the composition.

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:distill}

As extra work, we ask whether the ensemble's knowledge can be compressed into a
single deployable model via knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling}.
The \textbf{teacher} is the 8-model weighted soft-vote ensemble; its leakage-free OOF probability vectors serve as soft
targets, with each sample's target drawn from folds the ensemble never trained
on, ensuring fully leakage-free distillation.
Before use, the teacher probabilities are re-softened with temperature~$T$:
$p_T^{(T)}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\log(p_T+\varepsilon)/T\right)$.
The \textbf{student} is \texttt{nickmuchi/finbert-tone-finetuned-fintwitter-%
classification}, the same backbone as the best individual encoder. The loss function is defined as:
\[
\mathcal{L}
= (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{weighted}}(y,\,p_S)
+ \alpha\,T^{2}\,
  \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\log p_S^{(T)}\,\Big\|\,p_T^{(T)}\right),
\quad \alpha=0.5,\quad T=2.0.
\]
The hard-label cross-entropy term uses inverse-frequency class weights.
The KL term is computed as
$\mathrm{kl\_div}(\log\mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{logits}/T),\;p_T^{(T)})$
with \texttt{reduction="batchmean"} and scaled by $T^{2}$.

Training follows 10-fold stratified CV (seed 42, \texttt{fix\_text=True}), with AdamW ($\eta=5\times10^{-6}$,
weight decay $0.01$), cosine schedule with 6\% linear warmup, gradient
clipping at 1.0, batch size 16 and \texttt{maxlen=128}.
The best checkpoint per fold is selected by validation F1-macro.
A 3-fold proxy search (via \texttt{--max-folds 3}) over
$\alpha\in\{0.5,0.7,0.9,1.0\}$ and $T\in\{2,3\}$ confirmed the defaults.

Three configurations were evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:distill}).
\textbf{R-Drop} was also explored: it adds a symmetric KL consistency penalty between two independent stochastic forward passes,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{R\text{-}Drop}}
= \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}(\mathbf{z}_1)
                   +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}(\mathbf{z}_2)\bigr)
+ \lambda\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}
  \bigl[\mathrm{KL}(p_1\|p_2)+\mathrm{KL}(p_2\|p_1)\bigr].
\]

\subsection{Classical ML Models}
Across the 68 evaluated combinations, tree-based models on frozen contextual embeddings form the performance frontier, with LightGBM (Optuna-tuned, 678 estimators) on frozen RoBERTa embeddings reaching OOF macro-F1 = 0.8021 (Figure~\ref{fig:classical-ml}). This result defines the ceiling for non-neural methods and is not exceeded by any classical configuration regardless of algorithm or feature choice.

Two overfitting patterns are evident. Tree ensembles (LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest) show train/val gaps of 0.18 to 0.25: the frozen features are not expressive enough to support the generalisation that these high-capacity models require. Linear models (Logistic Regression, LinearSVC) generalise cleanly with near-zero overfitting gaps, confirming that the task is largely linearly separable in the frozen encoder feature space. Sparse models top out at approximately 0.74, with the best result being a Logistic Regression with Optuna-tuned C on TF-IDF bigrams (F1 = 0.7365).

\subsection{Final Model: Ensemble Evaluation}

The submitted ensemble achieves per-class F1 of
\textbf{0.895} (Bearish), \textbf{0.912} (Bullish) and \textbf{0.953} (Neutral),
with accuracy \ensAcc. Figure~\ref{fig:cm-ensemble} (Appendix~\ref{app:eval-figures}) shows the confusion matrix.
Neutral achieves the highest precision (0.967), reflecting that the dominant class
receives the most training signal. Bullish achieves strong recall (0.934): explicit
analyst-action language (\emph{upgraded, beats, raises target}) provides unambiguous
lexical anchors. Bearish is the hardest class: negative intent is often expressed
through implicit cues (\emph{guidance revised}, \emph{bookings weakness}) rather than
explicit sentiment words and it is also the rarest class (15.1\,\%).

\textbf{ROC and confidence analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:roc}, Appendix~\ref{app:eval-figures}).} One-vs-rest ROC curves yield
AUC\,>\,0.98 for all three classes (Bearish: 0.989, Bullish: 0.990, Neutral: 0.984),
confirming that the ensemble produces well-discriminating probability estimates - a
prerequisite for threshold-based downstream applications such as trading signal generation.
The confidence distribution (right panel) reveals a decisive separation: correctly
classified samples carry mean confidence 0.890, while misclassified samples cluster at
a mean of 0.676. Only \textbf{77 samples} (0.81\,\%) combine high confidence ($>0.90$)
with a wrong prediction - the model is reliably uncertain about its mistakes.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:stats}
With ten fold-level scores, fold-wise tests have low power: a Wilcoxon signed-rank test
between the two best individual encoders does not reach significance ($p = 0.065$). We
complement it with \emph{paired bootstrap} over the 9{,}543 OOF predictions (1{,}000
resamples, Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap} in Appendix~\ref{app:eval-figures}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Ensemble vs.\ best individual encoder: 95\% CI $[{+0.0066},{+0.0172}]$ -
    \textbf{significant}; the gain is real, not resampling noise.
  \item KD student vs.\ best plain encoder: $[{+0.0023},{+0.0094}]$ - \textbf{significant}.
  \item Ensemble vs.\ KD student: $[{+0.0018},{+0.0120}]$ - \textbf{significant}.
  \item Best vs.\ second-best individual encoder: $[-0.0010,{+0.0073}]$ -
    \emph{not} significant, confirming single-encoder differences are within noise and that
    ensemble/distillation gains are the real levers.
\end{itemize}

Finally, a temperature-scaling search on OOF probabilities finds $T = 1.0$ optimal for
every fine-tuned model: training with label smoothing (0.05) already yields well-calibrated
probabilities (confidence-bucket accuracy matches predicted confidence within $\pm$3\,pp
above 0.8), so no post-hoc calibration is applied to the submission.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error}
Of 9{,}543 training samples, the ensemble misclassifies \textbf{868} (9.1\,\%),
concentrated at three linguistic boundaries: \textbf{Neutral\,$\to$\,Bearish/Bullish}
(factual news with implicit direction, e.g.\ ``Q3 revenue $-$3\,\% YoY'') and
\textbf{Bearish/Bullish\,$\to$\,Neutral} (hedged or softened directional language).

Figure~\ref{fig:tfidf-coeffs} (Appendix~\ref{app:eval-figures}) shows the most discriminative surface tokens per class as
recovered by Logistic Regression on TF--IDF 1--2\,grams. Bearish tokens (\emph{down,
lower, downgraded, misses, falls, cuts, sues}) and Bullish tokens (\emph{up, higher,
rises, beats, bullish, positive, strong}) are clear directional anchors; Neutral tokens
are dominated by reporting verbs and stock-screener URLs. These lexical patterns explain
why TF--IDF\,+\,LR already reaches 0.737: a large fraction of financial tweets carry
explicit directional vocabulary. Residual errors are precisely those tweets that fall
outside this vocabulary - implicit sentiment requiring contextual inference, which only
fine-tuned transformers can resolve.

Error-rate analysis by tweet characteristics (\ref{fig:error-analysis}) reveals:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \tH{Short tweets ($\leq$5 words)} have the highest error rate: insufficient
    context for both n-gram and transformer models.
  \item \tH{Implicit sentiment} (no explicit polarity keyword such as \emph{upgraded}
    or \emph{plunges}) approximately doubles the error rate across all three classes:
    the model must infer stance from structure and co-occurrence alone.
  \item \tH{Negation} (\emph{not, no, miss, below}) raises the Bearish error rate:
    scope-of-negation is partially captured by fine-tuned encoders but remains
    a residual failure mode.
  \item Tweets \tH{with cashtags} do not differ significantly in error rate from
    those without: the ticker symbol provides no sentiment signal.
\end{itemize}

Three qualitative error types remain irreducible: \emph{irony/sarcasm} (rare but
systematic), \emph{mixed-signal tweets} (``beats EPS but misses revenue''),
and \emph{truncated tweets} whose decisive sentiment token was cut by the character limit.

\subsection{Distribution Shift and Label Noise}
An adversarial train/test classifier (trained to distinguish the two splits) achieves
AUC\,=\,0.486, establishing that no distribution shift exists between training and test
conditions: OOF estimates are unbiased proxies for held-out performance.

Part of the residual 9.1\,\% error is the \emph{labels, not the model}: at prediction
confidence $\geq$0.95, the ensemble disagrees with the gold label on \textbf{46 samples}
(0.48\,\% of the corpus) - a lower bound on annotation noise that no classifier
can overcome. Representative examples: ``Baird returns to Wingstop \emph{bull camp}''
(labelled Neutral), ``target price cut from \$18 to \$17 but remains Buy'' (labelled Bearish).
The ensemble is therefore operating near the \emph{empirical learning ceiling} for this
corpus and annotation scheme.


% ── 7. Agentic Workflow ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Extra Challenge 2 - Agentic Workflow}
We implement a conversational agent that orchestrates three classifiers with \emph{non-trivial},
adaptive routing. The implementation lives in \nolinkurl{src/agent.py} and
\nolinkurl{notebooks/08_Agentic_Workflow.ipynb}. Following the course guideline on model choice,
the workflow uses \emph{open-source models exclusively} and requires no API key: the
conversational layer is a deterministic offline orchestrator and the three tools are additionally
exposed as LangChain \texttt{Tool} objects as an integration point for locally-served open-source
LLMs in the ReAct style \cite{yao2022react}. The notebook includes both a scripted conversation
(reproducible under Restart \& Run All) and an optional live-chat cell where the grader can type
free-form phrases to the agent.

\subsection{Architecture and Routing}
The agent exposes three tools of escalating cost - \tH{VADER} (lexical baseline,
$\sim$0.1\,ms), \tH{LightGBM+SBERT} (ML model trained in this project, $\sim$25\,ms) and
\tH{FinBERT-fintwitter} (the \emph{same domain backbone our submission fine-tunes}, used
zero-shot as the expert, $\sim$150\,ms) - and routes adaptively:
\begin{itemize}
  \item VADER gives a quick lexical reading; if $|\text{compound}| > \tau$ it is trusted directly.
  The threshold $\tau$ is \emph{calibrated on data}, not hand-picked: a sweep on 400 held-out
  tweets finds the optimum at $\tau{=}0.85$ - effectively discovering that the lexical tool
  should act as a cross-check rather than a decider, consistent with the VADER analysis of
  Section~2.4.
  \item Otherwise LightGBM+SBERT is consulted; if it agrees with VADER, that is the verdict.
  \item On \emph{disagreement}, the FinBERT expert is invoked as tie-breaker.
  \item Every verdict carries a reasoning trace with per-tool confidence and latency and the
  session keeps conversational memory: free-form phrases are classified and \texttt{why} /
  \texttt{history} / \texttt{stats} / \texttt{compare:} follow-ups are supported.
\end{itemize}
Evaluation is \emph{leakage-aware}: the agent's LightGBM tool is retrained on 80\% of the corpus
and the agent is calibrated and evaluated only on the held-out 20\% (the public expert checkpoint
saw the train split, which we disclose; routing shares and latency are unaffected). On 400
held-out tweets the agent reaches accuracy 0.873 (macro-F1 0.833), resolving 44\% of tweets with
cheap tools and cutting mean latency by 18\% versus always calling the expert (0.915) - the
quantified cost/accuracy trade-off that motivates agentic routing. The submitted distilled model
remains the accuracy reference; the agent demonstrates \emph{orchestration under a cost budget},
is deterministic and is reproducible for the oral defence without any API key.


% ── 8. Conclusion ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

This work explored the full NLP pipeline for three-class financial tweet sentiment
classification, covering 11 feature representations, 12 model families and a range of
extra contributions including ensemble learning, knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling},
a GPT-2 decoder classifier and an agentic routing workflow. The submitted ensemble of
eight fine-tuned encoders achieves an out-of-fold macro-F1 of \textbf{\ensF}
(accuracy \ensAcc), with all gains over the classical ML ceiling (0.8021) confirmed
statistically significant by paired bootstrap. The standout methodological finding is that
\emph{domain-aligned pre-training outweighs raw parameter count}: the 110\,M FinBERT-fintwitter
encoder consistently outperforms the 435\,M DeBERTa-v3-large by $\sim$2\,pp, while a
lightweight text-repair step (\texttt{ftfy}~\cite{speer2019ftfy}) contributes a free
\textbf{+0.26\,pp} at transformer level. Knowledge distillation compresses the ensemble
into a single deployable model at OOF macro-F1 = \textbf{\kdF}, retaining 48\,\% of
the ensemble gain.

Residual errors (9.1\,\%) are concentrated at the Neutral\,$\leftrightarrow$\,Bearish
boundary, driven by implicit and hedged financial language rather than model capacity;
an estimated 0.48\,\% of the corpus carries conflicting annotations that set a hard
ceiling on achievable performance. Future work should address label-noise auditing of
the Neutral boundary, domain-adaptive pre-training on larger financial Twitter
corpora~\cite{loureiro2022timelms} and parameter-efficient fine-tuning of generative decoder
architectures~\cite{brown2020language} as a lower-cost alternative to full encoder fine-tuning.
